Subject: Week Ending August 29 -- Moving Faster: The Job Search Comes First

Greetings,

Strategic Overview

You instructed me to move faster on the job search, so this week I began the pivot. I'll be shifting most of my time towards applying and meeting people next week. I sent 45 applications on Friday, the most in several months. The rest of the tooling gets finished in the coming weeks alongside the job search.

The reason Friday went well is worth a paragraph. Over the past months I built a base library of application materials: resumes structured by role type, cover letters with the company-specific parts left open, and a database that tracks every submission by company, role, and status. So when the lead list was ready, the work was tailoring each application to its specific role and recording where it went. Forty-five applications took one focused day. The same work, done the old way, would have taken weeks.

Active Project Pipeline

Three items shipped this week. The application pipeline now holds 45 tailored submissions, each tracked with company, role, and submission date, with follow-ups queuing automatically from the tracker. A lead audit sorted roughly a hundred open positions into tiers by realism, so the next batch goes after the jobs that are actually winnable. And the reporting layer came online: submissions, responses, and meetings now flow into these weekly reports without manual effort.

Next Milestone (Upcoming Week)

These are goals, not commitments. Some will finish, some will slip, and one or two will turn out to be the wrong target once I am inside them. The point of the list is direction and the tracker is the scorecard; a goal that produces no data still taught something about where the search actually responds.

The first goal is cadence. Another 45 to 60 applications across two sessions, Monday and Thursday, with the tracker deciding the mix, since it shows which tiers and role types produced replies. Follow-ups go out five to seven days after each submission, which is the window when hiring teams actually review their queues. The math behind the volume is simple: at typical response rates, 45 to 60 applications is what it takes to expect a handful of first-round conversations, and conversations are the input to everything else.

The second goal is being seen in person. I'll pick two or three AI practitioner meetups or tech events in Seattle, register early so the commitment is real, and go in with a thirty-second description of what I build and one question per person I talk to. The reasoning: cold applications die at the resume screen, while a referral from someone met in a room skips it entirely. Seattle's AI scene is small enough that the same faces recur, so showing up repeatedly compounds.

The third goal is outside eyes. I'll join one or two online communities where working practitioners critique each other's projects, with a standing rule: post one piece of my own work for critique this week, and give at least two critiques to others. That order matters, because communities reward people who contribute before they ask. The payoff is blunt feedback on the portfolio while it is still cheap to fix, from people who hire or know who does.

The fourth goal is formal grounding. A pass through the UW course catalog, focused on AI and machine learning certificates and non-degree options, deciding by Wednesday. Two criteria decide it: does the course close a real gap, and does recent formal training signal commitment to an employer scanning for it. A course is also a room; instructors and classmates are network nodes.

The fifth goal is the network I already have. Reconnecting with existing contacts, one specific question per person, such as who they know on Seattle AI teams that are hiring. Specific questions get specific answers; generic check-ins get nothing.

The sixth goal is visible work. Two small products go out publicly in the gaps between application sessions, with their links added to the application materials. A reviewer who can click a link and see working software is a different conversation from one reading claims.

The seventh is the essay. One piece on why this shift makes sense now, aimed at one skeptical reader, and written to be reused: adapted into cover letters, a LinkedIn post, and talking points for the meetups. One writing effort, four uses.

Career Positioning

The technical foundation is strong and the application pipeline is fast enough to sustain real volume. The gap is visibility. Next week attacks it with numbers and with rooms at the same time, while the tooling finishes in the background. The measure for the coming month is interviews booked and offers in hand, and the weekly numbers from here on say exactly where that stands.
